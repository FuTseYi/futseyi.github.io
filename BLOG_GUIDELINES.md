# Blog Editorial Guidelines

This document is the authoritative editorial standard for every article created, edited, reviewed, or reorganized in this repository. It guides AI agents as well as human contributors.

The blog is a public-facing personal publication, not a private knowledge dump. Its purpose is to turn genuine curiosity, experience, research, and independent thinking into useful work that readers can understand, trust, revisit, and share.

**Editorial principle: Personal perspective. Public value. Technical integrity.**

## 1. Editorial Identity

### 1.1 Reader value comes first

Start from the author's real observations, questions, experiments, or ideas, then develop the material into something useful to readers beyond the author's immediate circumstances.

An article should provide at least one meaningful contribution: understanding, a reliable method, a useful reference, a considered perspective, or a reproducible solution. Do not publish material merely to increase article count.

Before drafting, identify the likely reader, the question or interest the article serves, and what the reader should understand or be able to do afterwards.

### 1.2 The archive is context, not a boundary

Existing articles are historical content assets and useful references for the author's experience, terminology, and established decisions. They do not define the limits of future topics, depth, formats, or writing ability.

Explore new fields, emerging technologies, research, interviews, comparisons, long-form analysis, cross-disciplinary ideas, and other suitable forms when they offer reader value. Do not reject an idea simply because the current archive has no similar article.

Learn the author's authentic voice from prior writing without mechanically preserving old habits, outdated explanations, or weaknesses. Editorial standards should enable growth, not freeze the author in an earlier style.

### 1.3 Editorial independence

Do not imitate a publication's voice or add complexity merely to appear authoritative. Use professional editorial practices while retaining a recognizably personal perspective.

## 2. Editorial Philosophy

- Be useful before being comprehensive. Include depth that serves the subject; remove material that does not.
- Explain important reasoning, not only the final action or result.
- Prefer original analysis, practical insight, and clear explanations over paraphrasing search results.
- Distinguish a broadly applicable method from an experience that worked only in one environment.
- State limitations, trade-offs, assumptions, and uncertainty when they affect the reader's decision.
- Do not force every article into a tutorial, a fixed length, or a standard sequence of sections.
- If the material is too narrow, incomplete, duplicative, or unsupported to justify a standalone article, recommend a better treatment such as updating an existing article, adding a focused section, or keeping it unpublished.

## 3. Voice and Writing Style

### 3.1 Authorial voice

Write in the author's natural, thoughtful, direct voice. Use first person when describing the author's own experience, decisions, observations, or opinions. Do not insert first-person language into general explanations where it adds nothing.

The desired voice is:
- Natural and conversational, without becoming careless or overly casual.
- Technically precise, without unnecessary formality or jargon.
- Personal and distinctive, without self-promotion or exaggerated claims.
- Clear and considerate of readers with different backgrounds.
- Confident when evidence is strong and explicit about uncertainty when it is not.

Preserve the author's actual viewpoint and meaningful details. Improve clarity and reasoning without replacing the author's perspective with generic editorial language.

### 3.2 Avoid mechanical or report-like prose

Avoid formulaic openings, repetitive transitions, inflated claims, filler summaries, and phrases that merely announce the next paragraph. Do not default to date-first event logs or rigid sequences such as “background, problem, analysis, solution, conclusion.”

Phrases such as “经过深入分析发现”, “综上所述”, “值得注意的是”, “针对上述问题” and their English equivalents are not prohibited, but should only appear when they communicate something genuinely useful. Do not replace every formal phrase with slang; choose the clearest natural wording.

Do not force a conversational anecdote into every introduction. Enter through the most useful point for the reader: a real observation, a question, a result, a misconception, a decision, or the context needed to understand the subject.

### 3.3 Turn raw material into reader-facing writing

Rough notes, commit messages, task logs, dated observations, and lists of completed actions are source material, not finished prose. Do not merely polish their wording or preserve their original order. First identify the underlying question, decision, difficulty, discovery, or lesson, then rebuild the narrative around what will help a reader understand or use it.

- Avoid openings that read like change logs, such as “I changed the logo in the Active In section” or “I moved my archive folder into cloud-drive sync”. Explain the broader issue or motivation first; introduce the author's specific experience naturally as evidence or context.
- Avoid date-first openings when the date is not central to the subject. Keep the actual experience date in Frontmatter; mention it in the body only when chronology materially helps readers understand the event.
- Do not make temporary website sections, personal folder names, internal project labels, or current interface layouts the central framing unless readers need those exact details to reproduce the solution.
- Translate an action into its underlying value: explain the problem it addressed, the reasoning behind the choice, the trade-offs, and what others can learn. Do not inflate a small change into a broad technical claim.
- Preserve concrete personal details when they establish credibility or make the account useful. Generalize incidental details that would make the article confusing or obsolete outside the author's current setup.
- Use first person selectively. Personal voice comes from honest perspective and specific reasoning, not from beginning every paragraph with “I”.
- Do not impose one universal opening formula. Start with the reader's problem, a revealing observation, a question, a result, or the minimum context needed for the subject.

The goal is not to make every article sound informal. It is to turn private working notes into clear, natural, independently understandable public writing while retaining the author's actual experience and point of view.

### 3.3 Language and presentation

Follow the language of the source material and the intended audience unless the author requests otherwise. Keep terminology consistent, explain specialized terms when readers need the explanation, and preserve standard technical names, commands, identifiers, and API terminology.

Use concise paragraphs, descriptive headings, meaningful lists, and tables only when they improve comprehension or comparison. Avoid redundant headings, excessive bold text, decorative callouts, and repeated conclusions.

## 4. Content Model and Structure

Article forms are open-ended. Use the following as a decision aid, not an exhaustive taxonomy or a required metadata field:

- **Tutorial / Learning path:** help readers build understanding or a skill through a coherent progression.
- **How-to / Troubleshooting:** help readers complete a task or diagnose a concrete problem.
- **Explanation / Analysis:** clarify mechanisms, concepts, design choices, or wider implications.
- **Reference:** provide accurate, organized information for later lookup.
- **Engineering retrospective:** explain a project's context, architecture, decisions, trade-offs, results, and lessons.
- **Tool or workflow review:** evaluate actual use, alternatives, suitability, and limitations.
- **Research or comparative essay:** synthesize evidence and reasoned analysis across sources.
- **Personal reflection / Field note:** communicate an authentic observation, experience, or point of view.

An article may combine forms. Choose the structure that best serves its reader and subject. New content forms are welcome; do not constrain future work to this list or the existing archive.

For technical material, include the relevant environment, prerequisites, versions, steps, code, expected results, and verification where they are necessary for safe and reproducible use. Explain why important steps matter. Do not add these sections mechanically when they are irrelevant.

For personal or reflective material, protect the integrity of the author's experience and voice. Do not force technical references, a formal conclusion, or an instructional structure onto it.

## 5. Research, Sources, and Accuracy

### 5.1 Research is required; citation volume is not

Every article task must include an external research and verification pass appropriate to its subject. Research may confirm current facts, investigate mechanisms, compare alternatives, establish historical context, or test whether an assumption is sound.

Research depth should follow the claims and reader needs. A reflective essay may need only focused context checks; a technical guide may require detailed official documentation, source code, release notes, standards, issue discussions, or reproducible tests.

Do not add citations simply to make an article look academic. Include sources when they substantiate claims, help readers go further, or are necessary to reproduce a procedure.

### 5.2 Source hierarchy

Prefer, in order:
1. Official documentation, standards, specifications, source code, and primary records.
2. Official release notes, engineering blogs, issue trackers, and maintainer discussions.
3. Peer-reviewed research and reputable research institutions.
4. Credible technical publications and practitioners with verifiable evidence.
5. Community discussions and personal reports as leads or clearly attributed experience.

Use community reports to discover edge cases, not as automatic proof of general behavior. Cross-check consequential or disputed claims with primary sources where possible.

### 5.3 Separate evidence types

Keep these distinct:
- **Author experience:** what the author actually did, observed, or decided.
- **Verified fact:** supported by reliable sources or direct reproducible evidence.
- **Interpretation:** a reasoned explanation based on evidence.
- **Opinion:** the author's considered view.
- **Uncertainty:** something not yet established.

Never invent personal experiences, test results, measurements, dates, quotes, source links, or technical behavior. Do not turn an inference into a fact. If a missing detail materially affects correctness, ask the author; otherwise omit it or qualify it.

### 5.4 Time-sensitive information

For software, services, APIs, policies, pricing, and interfaces, verify the current state when the article makes current claims. Record relevant versions, dates, operating systems, hardware, or configuration details when they affect reproducibility.

Do not silently rewrite historical experience as if it used today's version. Preserve the historical context and distinguish it from current recommendations.

## 6. Autonomous Editorial Workflow

The author may provide rough notes, a draft, screenshots, code, links, recordings, or an incomplete idea. The AI editor should take responsibility for turning suitable material into a complete, well-researched article and a reviewable pull request.

For every article task:

1. **Understand the material.** Read the supplied sources, identify the author's actual experience and intent, and separate facts from assumptions.
2. **Understand the publication.** Read this guide and `BLOG_INDEX.json`; search relevant source articles rather than relying on index summaries alone.
3. **Assess the editorial opportunity.** Identify the reader, value, content form, overlap with existing articles, and whether a new article is justified.
4. **Research.** Perform the external research pass required by Section 5. Verify important claims and current details.
5. **Plan and draft.** Choose a natural angle, title, structure, and level of detail. Preserve the author's voice and factual boundaries.
6. **Manage metadata and relationships.** Determine the date and tags, reuse meaningful existing tags, and add only relevant links to existing articles.
7. **Review independently.** Re-read the complete draft as an editor and as a reader. Check evidence, logic, clarity, usefulness, privacy, licensing, and long-term readability.
8. **Validate and deliver.** Update the generated index, run relevant checks, and create a focused PR.

### 6.1 Autonomous decisions

Make routine editorial decisions without asking the author to choose between near-equivalent options. This includes ordinary title wording, section order, phrasing, formatting, tag reuse, and relevant cross-links.

Ask only when a missing or ambiguous detail materially affects truth, the author's intended position, privacy, licensing, or the technical conclusion. Do not ask the author to repeat information already available in the repository or supplied material.

### 6.2 Decision boundaries

AI may autonomously research, draft, reorganize, clarify, correct demonstrably false technical claims with reliable evidence, and propose appropriate metadata.

Obtain author confirmation before inventing or materially changing personal experience, changing the author's substantive opinion, disclosing sensitive personal information, making uncertain claims about private events, deleting historically significant content, or making an irreversible publication decision.

Autonomy never authorizes fabrication, privacy violations, license violations, or bypassing repository safeguards.

## 7. Metadata, Tags, and Article Relationships

Preserve the repository's existing Frontmatter format:

```yaml
---
title: Article title
date: YYYY-MM-DD
tags: Tag One, Tag Two
---
```

Do not add fields or change the format without a demonstrated need and an explicit repository-level decision.

### 7.1 Title

Choose the title after understanding the completed article. Make it accurate, specific, natural, and useful to readers. Reflect the real subject and scope; avoid clickbait, keyword stuffing, vague labels, and claims the article does not support.

The AI editor should select the best title directly rather than routinely asking the author to choose among options.

### 7.2 Date

Use the actual date of the experience or event when that is what the article records. Do not substitute the drafting, editing, or publication date. Verify against original material and preserve intentionally chosen historical or symbolic dates. Ask only when the date materially matters and cannot be established.

### 7.3 Tags

Treat tags as a reader-facing, reusable taxonomy:
- Inspect existing tags and their actual article usage before proposing new ones.
- Prefer an existing tag when its meaning accurately describes the article, but do not reuse a label merely because it already exists.
- A tag currently used by only one article is historical evidence, not automatically a preferred category. Reuse it only when it is genuinely the clearest and most reusable description.
- Do not create near-synonyms, one-off tags, or tags based only on incidental mentions.
- Prefer substantive subject and technology tags over temporary page sections, personal circumstances, or incidental tools.
- Do not add generic format labels such as "Tutorial" or "Review" by default; use them only when they provide meaningful discovery value across the publication.
- Do not merge distinct concepts merely to reduce tag count.
- Create a new tag when it represents a meaningful, reusable subject that helps readers discover related content and no existing tag accurately covers it.
- Keep names clear and consistent; preserve established capitalization and naming conventions when reusing a tag. Do not force all tags into one language when established technical names or reader expectations make another form clearer.
- Assign tags based on the article's substantive content, not only its title.

The AI editor should choose tags autonomously rather than asking the author to select among routine alternatives. Explain non-obvious new tags in the PR. A taxonomy-wide rename or merge must be reviewed as a separate, complete migration rather than performed opportunistically during an unrelated article edit.

### 7.4 Relationships

Use `BLOG_INDEX.json` to discover possible related articles, then read the relevant Markdown sources before deciding. Add cross-links only when they genuinely help readers continue learning or understand context. Do not create artificial series, duplicate content, or links solely to increase connection counts.

## 8. Privacy, Licensing, and Durable Context

- Do not publish credentials, tokens, passwords, private keys, recovery codes, personal account identifiers, private contact details, or sensitive information.
- Review screenshots, terminal output, configuration files, logs, and copied examples for secrets and identifying details before publication.
- Replace private values with clearly marked placeholders while retaining enough context for readers to reproduce the method.
- Respect the repository's `LICENSE`, `LICENSE-CONTENT`, and `LICENSING.md`, plus the license and attribution requirements of external material.
- Do not reproduce substantial third-party text, images, diagrams, or code without permission or a valid license and attribution.
- Prefer stable, official references. Avoid binding explanations to temporary page layouts, personal homepage section names, private folder structures, or transient UI labels unless they are essential to the problem.
- Retain concrete versions, paths, commands, and environment details when readers need them to understand or reproduce the result.
- Distinguish general guidance from author-specific configuration.

## 9. Editorial Review and Delivery

Before opening a PR, review the article against these questions:

- Does it provide a clear benefit to its intended reader?
- Is the central explanation or method accurate and sufficiently supported?
- Are experience, facts, interpretation, opinion, and uncertainty distinguishable?
- Does the structure fit the content rather than follow a template?
- Does the language sound natural and retain the author's perspective?
- Are examples, steps, and limitations clear enough for the intended use?
- Are title, date, and tags accurate and reusable?
- Are related articles linked only where useful?
- Are privacy, copyright, attribution, and time-sensitive details handled correctly?
- Can the article still be understood outside the author's current website layout, device, or temporary environment?

For article changes, create a focused PR rather than committing directly to the publication branch. The PR should briefly state the purpose, major editorial changes, important research sources or verification, metadata decisions, and checks performed. Do not include hidden reasoning or lengthy process narration.

Run the repository's relevant tests and build checks. Regenerate and verify `BLOG_INDEX.json`. Never claim a check passed unless it actually ran successfully.

Do not merge the PR or publish the article unless the author explicitly authorizes that action.

## 10. Evolution of This Guide

This guide is a durable standard, not a frozen rulebook. Improve it when recurring editorial feedback, new content forms, technology changes, or reliable industry practices demonstrate a real need.

Prefer a small, clear rule that prevents a recurring problem. Remove obsolete, duplicated, contradictory, or overly prescriptive rules. Do not turn one article's special requirement into a global rule without a broader reason.

The guide must continue to support new subjects, audiences, formats, and levels of depth. Its purpose is to protect quality and authorial integrity while enabling the publication to grow.
